## Part 1 — What the session teaches

### Why it matters
In school a paper "must be five pages." In the real world: *explain it to me in
seven seconds, or I'm out.* If someone can't quickly grasp what you do, they
don't buy, invest, collaborate, or write about you. So writing is **translating
and deleting**.

### What good copy is
- Clear and concise — no re-reading, no "wait, what?"
- Focused on the audience, not on you.
- Anticipates the reader's questions and answers them.
- The test: **a description lands when someone else can explain what you do in
  their own words.**

### The worked example (CoatCheck), three versions
- **Buzzword version** — "an AI-enabled, IoT-integrated outerwear logistics
  platform leveraging a distributed network of smart storage infrastructure…"
  Impressive-sounding, says nothing.
- **Vague version** — "helps travelers travel smarter and enjoy a better
  journey… focus on what matters." Pleasant, empty.
- **Good version** — "a self-service locker system that stores the things
  travelers won't need at their destination. Available at major airports, you
  reserve and manage storage in an app, so you don't drag bulky coats or a
  second bag on vacation." Concrete, plain, differentiated.

### Decide the basics before you write
- **Who** (audience) — the specific person, not "customers".
- **What it is** (product type) — the plain thing it does, no jargon.
- **What they get** (outcome) — the result; what changed or improved.
- **What sets it apart** (secret sauce) — the honest difference.
- Then decide **how**: the voice (which words to use / avoid) and the one idea
  the reader must walk away with.

### The description templates
- **Short:** [Company] helps [audience] [solve problem] by [solution].
- **Long:** [Company] is a [product type] that helps [audience] [get outcome] by
  [solution]. What sets it apart is [secret sauce].
- **Differentiator endings:** "Unlike most [product type], it…" / "Unlike the
  usual alternative, [company]…" / "Where others [do the common thing],
  [company]…"

### Tweak like a DJ
One brief, different readers — move the faders. Turn *feeling* up for the
public, down for an investor; turn *market / numbers* up for an investor. The
content stays; you change what's loud.

### Red writing flags (the AI giveaways)
- **Buzzwords:** revolutionary, cutting-edge, game-changing, synergy, leverage,
  disrupt.
- **Vague claims:** significant improvements, enhance productivity, drive
  results, optimize performance.
- **Hype words:** seamlessly, effortlessly, unprecedented, transformative,
  unlock, empower.
- **Features dressed as benefits:** "we use AI" (so what?).
- **Unnecessary adjectives / adverbs:** highly effective, extremely powerful.
- **Uniform sentence length** — every sentence the same shape.
- **From the "top giveaways" list:** overuse of em-dashes; "not only X, but also
  Y"; rule-of-three obsession; false ranges ("5–7 tips" when no range exists);
  inflated importance ("pivotal moment"); generic enthusiasm without specifics;
  total lack of personal detail; bolded bullet titles followed by redundant
  sentences; Title Case for Emphasis; random emoji.

### But — don't overcut
None of these is banned; they're patterns to *notice*. What makes a reader stop
is the **same pattern over and over**. The real test: **are readers noticing the
pattern, or the point?**

### Why AI writes this way
It predicts the "most likely" next word — the average, expected one — which is
why it reads generic. And it doesn't look back at the bigger picture, so it
repeats itself and leans on safe patterns.

### The method
- **The sandwich** — AI is the bread (pressure-test your thinking before you
  draft; tighten and catch inconsistencies after). **You are the filling**
  (write the draft — your point, your words; messy is fine). Nobody orders a
  sandwich for the bread.
- **Sparring partner, not ghostwriter** — make AI explain its choices: "why
  this?", "what are you assuming?", "what's the weakest part?", "where could
  anyone have written this?", "what would make someone stop reading?"
- Two ready prompts are provided: one to flag AI patterns in existing copy, one
  to rewrite a jargon-heavy description into the fill-in-the-blank framework.

## Part 2 — Additions for VortexDeep

Extensions that fit the session without contradicting it.
(★ = already applied in the v11 slide pass.)

- **Show, don't claim.** Replace an adjective with a specific noun or number.
  "20 years in industrial operations", "break-even at 6–8 clients", "a live
  Swiss SME pilot" beat any adjective. ★ (the deck runs on specifics)
- **Two questions that kill weak sentences:** "So what?" (forces a benefit) and
  "Compared to what?" (forces a differentiator). If a line survives both, keep
  it.
- **The competitor test.** Could a competitor put their name on this exact
  sentence? If yes, it says nothing about you. "We're passionate about quality"
  fails; "a person approves every step before anything is sent" passes. ★
- **Lead with the reader's problem, not your solution.** Same principle as
  "focused on the audience, not on you", applied to structure. ★ (Day-5 script)
- **Cut the hedges:** just, really, very, quite, actually, a bit, sort of, "in
  order to" (= "to"). ★ (tightening pass)
- **Strong verbs over verb-plus-noun:** "decide", not "make a decision";
  "helps", not "provides assistance with".
- **Delete your first sentence.** It's often throat-clearing; the real point is
  usually sentence two.
- **Vary length on purpose.** The fix for uniform rhythm isn't randomness — a
  short line lands harder right after a long one. ★ (em-dash pass)
- **Beat the curse of knowledge.** Explain it to a smart friend outside your
  field. Same as "someone else explains it in their own words".
- **Working with AI:** give it voice samples (two paragraphs you'd be proud of),
  a banned-words list (your own red flags), and ask for three variants then
  Frankenstein the best bits. Ask it to critique before it rewrites — otherwise
  it just launders the average.

## Quick self-check before anything ships
1. Could someone outside the field explain it back in their own words?
2. Does every sentence survive "So what?" and "Compared to what?"
3. Could a competitor sign their name to any line? (If yes, rewrite it.)
4. Is any single pattern repeating enough to be noticed? (em-dash, triads,
   uniform length, bolded lead-ins.)
5. Is there at least one specific, checkable detail — a number, a name, a fact?
