# VortexDeep — LinkedIn & Targeting Starter Kit

Three tools: (1) a niche-scoring worksheet to pick your wedge + segment on paper,
(2) a discovery-listening guide — warm conversations, never cold calls, never
selling, and (3) three ready-to-post LinkedIn drafts.

---

## PART 1 — Niche-scoring worksheet

Score each candidate segment 1–5 on five factors. Total out of 25.
**20+ = strong. Under 15 = probably not your first move.**

The five factors (the ones the market itself uses):
1. **Budget** — can they afford CHF 1,000–5,000/month? (High revenue per customer = yes.)
2. **Repetitive pain** — obvious, recurring, time-eating tasks?
3. **Weak status quo** — current solution painful, costly, or non-existent?
4. **Can't DIY** — no in-house AI/tech skill to do it themselves?
5. **Your access** — do you have a *warm* way in (network, past industry, region)?

I've pre-scored the first two from the research + your profile; **you fill the rest,
and correct my guesses — especially column 5, which only you know.**

**How to use it:** the winner is usually *high total AND a 4–5 in "your access."* A
perfect market fit you can't reach loses to a good fit you can reach warm. Pick ONE.

**Free ways to fill in columns 1–4 for any segment (the research method):**
- Google Trends + search autocomplete — what people type.
- Competitors' service & case-study pages — what they sell = what sells.
- Most-used Make / n8n / Zapier templates — popular template = wanted workflow.
- **Job ads** — a firm hiring someone to sort inboxes / enter invoices is a firm that
  would pay to automate it. Scan roles → you get both the task (wedge) and a target list.
- Reddit / LinkedIn / forum threads — the buyer's own words for the pain.

## PART 2 — Discovery-listening guide (NOT a cold call)

**What this is:** a warm, curious conversation with someone you already know, or reach
through a warm introduction. You ask nothing, you sell nothing — you learn how they
work. Fully Zen: listening, not pitching. Works in person, over coffee, in writing,
or as a LinkedIn message thread.

**Opening (sets the no-pitch tone):**
> "I'm mapping how small [engineering/fiduciary] teams actually spend their week —
> not selling anything, just trying to understand the real bottlenecks. Could I ask
> you a few questions about how yours runs?"

**The five questions:**
1. Walk me through a normal week — where does your team's time *actually* go?
2. Which recurring task feels the most repetitive or annoying?
3. When something comes in — an inquiry, an invoice, a request — what happens, step by step?
4. Where do things slip through, get missed, or get redone?
5. Have you tried any tools for this? What worked, and what put you off?

**Close (no ask, leaves a door open):**
> "This is genuinely helpful — thank you. Mind if I follow up if I come across
> something that fits what you described?"

**What you're listening for:** a task that is (a) repetitive, (b) triggered predictably,
(c) currently eats real hours, and (d) has a measurable payoff if automated. That's a
wedge. Three of these conversations will tell you more than a month of guessing.

## PART 3 — Three LinkedIn posts (ready to adapt & post)

Posting notes: pick ONE reader per post. Post 1–2×/week. Reply to every comment in the
first hour. No email gates, no "DM me for the PDF" — the post *is* the value. A soft
"what's your experience?" question earns comments, and comments earn reach.

---

### POST A — speaks to your warm audience (engineering / technical / ops SMEs)

Most small firms I speak with don't have an "AI problem."

They have a "too much of the week disappears into repetitive admin" problem.

Inquiries to sort. Quotes to draft. Documents to file. The same handful of tasks, over
and over — usually landing on your most experienced people, who should be doing the
actual work.

So the useful question isn't "how do we adopt AI." It's: which repetitive slice can we
hand off — while every decision that matters still gets a human's eyes?

That line is everything. Automating the sorting and drafting is easy. Automating the
judgement is where things go wrong. So we don't. The machine prepares; a person
approves; nothing goes out on its own.

If you run a small technical or professional team in Switzerland — what's the one
repetitive task you'd hand off first, if you could keep full control of the outcome?

Genuinely curious. Drop it below. 👇

---

### POST B — the differentiator (the "API gap," in plain words)

A quiet reason automation "doesn't work" for a lot of Swiss SMEs:

Most automation tools connect software through APIs — the digital plug that lets two
systems talk to each other. Works beautifully… when the plug exists.

But much of the software Swiss businesses actually run on has no open plug, or a
gated, costly one. So the standard "connect app A to app B" approach hits a wall
exactly where the pain is worst.

There are only two ways past that wall:
– expensive certified middleware, or
– AI that operates the software the way a person would — with a human checking each step.

The second is where we work. Carefully — because one wrong entry in accounting isn't a
rounding error, it's a problem. So a person approves before anything is saved or sent.

It's slower to build than a flashy demo. It's also the part that actually holds up.

If you've been told your systems "can't be automated because they don't talk to each
other" — that's often not the end of the story. Just not the easy version of it.

---

### POST C — the POV / trust post ("assistant, not autopilot")

An unpopular opinion for an automation company to hold:

Some things should not be automated.

The judgement calls. The ambiguous cases. The client who needs a human, not a workflow.
The final sign-off on anything that touches money, law, or people.

We build automation for the repetitive slice *around* those things — the reading,
sorting, drafting, filing — so the people in your business have more room for the parts
that genuinely need them.

"Assistant, not autopilot" isn't a limitation we apologise for. It's the whole point.

If an automation partner promises to run your business without you in the loop — ask
them who's accountable when it's wrong.
